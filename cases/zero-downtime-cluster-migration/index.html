Zero-downtime migration to new Kubernetes clusters

Moving a whole platform onto new clusters is the kind of change that goes wrong at 2 a.m. Here is how I did it with no downtime: a blue-green cutover at the cluster level, an internal DNS switch, and scale-to-zero as a one-command rollback.

Rebuilding clusters is only half the job. Once the new clusters exist, every workload still has to move onto them, and "move production to a different cluster" is exactly the sentence that makes people schedule a midnight maintenance window. I wanted no window and no downtime, so I treated the whole thing as a blue-green deployment, just at the level of the cluster instead of a single service.

The strategy: blue-green at the cluster level

The new clusters ran in parallel with the legacy ones. Nothing was deleted until the new side was proven. The sequence:

  • Stand up the workloads on the new clusters while the legacy clusters keep serving all live traffic.
  • Cut over by pointing internal DNS for each migrated app at the new clusters. Traffic shifts at the DNS layer, not by redeploying callers.
  • Scale the legacy workloads to zero rather than deleting them. They stay as a warm rollback: if anything looks wrong, scaling the old side back up and flipping DNS back is a one-command revert.
  • Decommission only after soak, once the new side has run clean long enough to trust.

What actually had to move

A platform is more than its stateless pods. The migration covered the whole surface:

  • Applications — the stateless services, redeployed onto the new clusters.
  • Cloud Functions, schedulers and task queues — the event-driven and cron-driven glue that is easy to forget until something stops firing.
  • Firestore data — the stateful part, migrated to the new project so the apps on the new clusters read from the right place.
  • Scale schedules — the up/down scaling windows for non-production, recreated so the new clusters cost the same to idle as the old ones.

Scale to zero, do not delete

The single most valuable move in a cluster migration is keeping the old workloads at zero replicas instead of deleting them. It costs almost nothing to park an idle deployment, and it turns rollback from a rebuild into a one-line scale command plus a DNS flip. Delete only after the new side has earned your trust.

The parts people forget

The migration is not done when traffic flows. Two cleanups mattered:

  • Monitoring that still probes the old hosts. After the cutover, blackbox probes were checking the legacy internal hostnames and showing a wall of false "down" alerts. Repointing them at the migrated hosts was what actually made the migration "finished".
  • Tooling left behind on the legacy clusters. Cost agents and other cluster-scoped add-ons had to be uninstalled cleanly (Helm releases plus leftover CRDs and namespaces), so the empty clusters really were empty before teardown.

What I would tell someone starting this

  • Treat a cluster move as blue-green. Run both in parallel, cut over at the DNS layer, and never delete the old side until the new one is proven.
  • Scale the old workloads to zero, do not delete them. That is your rollback, and it is nearly free to keep.
  • Inventory the whole surface first: functions, schedulers, task queues and stateful data, not just the obvious pods.
  • Finish the job. Repoint monitoring at the new hosts and clean up the old clusters, or you will chase phantom alerts for weeks.

Decisions and next steps

A retrospective view of the approach and its limits. The next improvement is a proposal, not a delivered result.

Alternative and rationale

An in-place move reduces parallel capacity; parallel clusters enable destination validation before shifting traffic.

Trade-off

Parallel operation adds temporary cost, DNS/cache behavior and state-synchronization risks.

What I would improve next

Next improvement: record per-service acceptance, rollback time and the conditions for safely removing legacy capacity.
